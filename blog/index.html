In Defense of the Poor Image

Hito Steyerl, 2011

I understand the tension Steyerl describes in many ways. As I work in post on a project, everything comes down to delivery. What gets delivered in full quality, what gets compressed, what circulates as low res that should never ever be shared. I assumed that was a technical hierarchy, but now I'm aware of it as a value hierarchy as well. Whose footage and whose equipment counts as legitimate? Usually those who are well funded and connected.

In section 5, she talks about the "new aura," and that idea stuck with me quite a lot. It's assumed that quality is what gives documentary its weight, that clean, high res, well produced and high budgeted films are what win the audience's trust and therefore attendance. But some of the most affecting footage I've worked with was the opposite: found footage, shaky, low resolution. That footage isn't "unprofessional." Its very existence defies professionalism. It shows that there was no one around producing, managing that moment. Steyerl's point that the poor image's aura comes from its transience rather than its permanence puts a theory behind something very instinctual, especially as an editor.

Her ambivalence about the poor image in section four is the part I find hardest to sit with. When filmmakers and editors take dense, hard-to-access footage and turn it into something watchable, I think they're doing something good. Something closer to Espinosa's "imperfect cinema" than to capitalism's obsession with speed and turnover. But Steyerl doesn't believe in that distinction. The same platforms that help marginalized stories reach people are also built to reward speed over depth, quick previews over real attention.

The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction

Walter Benjamin, 1935

I'm interested in Benjamin's idea of aura. Right after reading Benjamin's essay, I went to a yoga class and the idea of aura became even more concrete. In section IX, he talks about a stage actor's aura being "tied to his presence; there can be no replica of it." A live performance has something that a filmed one structurally can't have, no matter how good the camera work is, because the performance and the audience's presence are happening together, once, unrepeatably. The instructor seemed like he was on the verge of some kind of breakdown. He was blasting music, insisting everyone sing along, scream, jump, let loose. People were mostly still and uncomfortable. By the time I left, it felt like I had just witnessed a piece of performance art, one with a very specific, unrepeatable aura. No description or video of it could actually recreate what that room felt like while it was happening. It only existed once, for the people who were physically there for it.

I like Benjamin's point that collective, distracted reception, i.e. watching a film, isn't necessarily a loss compared to quiet individual contemplation. There's something powerful about absorbing an experience together with other people, even in a distracted or casual way, rather than alone and fully concentrated. If people can take something in collectively, like a movie, music, ideas or a story, it feels like it makes collective action, or at least a collective feeling, more possible. Benjamin seems to be arguing that this shift, from solitary awe to shared, distracted experience, isn't art's decline. It's just a different, newer way of being moved by something, together instead of alone.

I don't really agree with Benjamin when he treats the decay of aura as a mostly positive, democratizing shift. Even he can't fully commit to that framing. He describes the aura in early photographic portraits as having a "melancholy, incomparable beauty," which isn't how you'd describe something you were glad to see disappear. I think aura is more personal and subjective than his essay allows for. It's not just about whether something is one-of-a-kind or infinitely copyable, it's about the physical effort, thought, and feeling an artist poured into making it, and that can register differently for every single person who encounters it. Two people standing in front of the same original work of art might have totally different experiences of its "aura," which suggests aura never purely came from the object itself — it's actually something more like the relationship between the object and the person standing in front of it.
